## Grounding Story 01 - The Extra Peso
When Tino was six, his mother began sending him to the bakery before school because he could be trusted to return with exact change.

It was not a large responsibility, though in their house it was treated as one. The bakery opened at five, and by six there was already a line of tricycle drivers, office clerks, and children in uniforms that were too new in June and too faded by January. Tino would stand there with a cloth purse, repeating the order under his breath: six pandesal, one loaf, no sweet bread unless discounted.

On a Tuesday in August, the woman at the counter gave him one peso too much. It was an ordinary mistake, one of the kind adults make when they are doing three things at once. Tino noticed at once. He noticed because he had sorted coins the night before by year and edge pattern and had memorized how seven pesos felt in his hand as opposed to six.

He stood at the end of the counter and waited for the woman to finish with two men arguing over cigarette change. Then he said, very clearly, that she had overpaid him.

At first she smiled the way adults smile when they think a child is performing. Then she counted again and saw she had, in fact, given him an extra coin.

"Very honest," she said, and pinched his cheek. She said it loudly enough that the line looked over.

A man behind him laughed and said, "This one will be an accountant." Another said, "No, lawyer. He already likes trouble."

The woman put the peso back in the drawer, then gave him a free sugar biscuit as if honesty needed sugar to be complete.

At school, Tino told the story exactly three times, each retelling cleaner than the last. By recess it had become, in his own account, less about honesty and more about detection. He explained to his seatmate that most people handled money carelessly and that errors were common if one did not maintain an internal ledger.

His seatmate nodded and then asked if he wanted to trade half a sandwich for the biscuit.

Tino said no, because he had not brought the biscuit to trade. He had brought it to preserve as evidence of the event.

By lunch, the biscuit had broken inside his bag.

That afternoon, their teacher asked the class to work in pairs on a worksheet. Tino corrected his partner's answers before the boy had finished writing them. He did this quickly and without apology, because the errors were obvious to him and time was short. The boy pushed the paper away and said he would do it himself. Tino, thinking this was stubbornness and not injury, completed both sets alone.

When school ended, the teacher kept Tino for a minute and told him he was very bright but must learn not to make classmates feel small.

He walked home slower than usual, not because he disagreed, and not because he agreed, but because he did not understand the mechanics of it. He could see, in memory, the exact sequence of what had happened. He could not see where the damage entered.

That evening, his mother asked why he was quiet at dinner.

Tino said the day had been efficient.

His mother looked at him for a long moment, then passed him the fish and asked him to hand the soy sauce to his younger sister first. He did, though he had reached for it before she did.

Years later, people would say Tix liked being right, and that was true in the way weather reports are true. It did not explain why, even as a child, he could return the extra peso without hesitation and still fail to recognize, until too late, what he had taken from someone else.

## Grounding Story 02 - The Arithmetic Cup
At nine, Tino Ramos had handwriting the teachers trusted. It was narrow, upright, and looked as if it had never been surprised by anything.

In July, Class 4-B began the monthly Arithmetic Cup. The cup was tin, plated once in something brighter, and missing one handle. It lived on top of the filing cabinet beside the attendance books. On Fridays, Ma'am Sison brought it down and set it on her desk with care out of proportion to its value.

The rules were written on cartolina and pinned to the wall:
`1) One answer only.`
`2) No corrections after the bell.`
`3) Checker's mark is final.`

Because Tino could add quickly and did not round up in his head, he was made checker.

He did not object. He liked columns. He liked the certainty of totals. He did not like the noise that came before totals, but he accepted it as part of the procedure.

By the third Friday, the class had formed a mild superstition about the cup. Whoever won it, they said, would top the quarter exam. This belief had no evidence and therefore strong support.

That month, the likely winner was Renzo de Vera, who answered before anyone else and spoke with the confidence of a child who had older brothers to be loud for him at home. Tino was second. He would not have said this aloud, but he knew it.

On the last round, Ma'am Sison gave ten multiplication items in sequence. The class wrote on scrap paper and raised slates when ready. Item eight was `7 x 16`. Renzo wrote `102`, crossed it out, and wrote `112` beside it. He lifted his slate exactly as the handbell rang.

Tino marked item eight wrong in his ledger.

Ma'am Sison came down the rows, checking quickly. At Renzo's desk, she paused.
"He corrected. That's fine," she said.

Tino, seated at the checker's table in front, said, "Rule two says no corrections after the bell."

He did not raise his voice. He said it as if reading weather.

The class went quiet in the particular way children go quiet when they suspect a punishment may become interesting.

Ma'am Sison looked at the wall cartolina, then at Tino, then at Renzo, who had already begun to smile as if the matter were solved.
"Tino, did he change it before the bell?" she asked.

Tino looked at his sheet. He had written a small red dot in the margin at the bell, as he always did, so he could remember where the time ended. Renzo's final answer sat after the dot.

"After," he said.

Ma'am Sison stood with her chalk in hand for a moment longer than anyone expected. Then she erased Renzo's point from the board.

The final tally put Renzo and Tino tied. By house practice, the checker could not win ties. The cup went to a girl named Maris, one point below, because she had no deductions and no disputes.

At recess, Renzo told two boys by the window that some people preferred rules because they were poor sports. Tino heard this and took no visible notice. He opened his lunch and ate half a boiled egg with salt folded into paper.

Maris came over carrying the cup as if it were hot.
"Can you check if they spelled my name right?" she asked.

On the masking tape stuck to the base, someone had written `Marris`.

Tino said, "Two r's."

She looked at him, uncertain whether this was correction or accusation. Then she laughed, peeled the tape off, and handed him her marker.

He rewrote it as `Maris`, left-justified.

After class, Ma'am Sison called him to the desk. She did not sound angry. She sounded tired in a practical way.

"You were correct," she said. "But sometimes you can let small things pass."

Tino said, "If rule two can pass, then rule three is not final."

She pressed her lips together, the way adults do when they are deciding whether a child has answered or merely continued.
"You are very good at this," she said. "Try to be good with people also."

At home, his mother asked why he was carrying the cup. He explained that he was fixing the label and had forgotten to return it. She rinsed rice, looked at the taped base, and said, "Be careful you don't become the kind of person everyone needs and nobody invites."

He set the cup on the table and checked the new label again for alignment. It was straight. The tin was scratched where the handle had broken years before, a clean oval edge like a missing ear.

The next morning, he returned it before first bell. He placed it on the cabinet facing front, with the name visible, and stepped down from the chair.

By lunch, three classmates had already begun calling him `Checker` as if it were a title and not a temporary duty. He answered when called. He did not correct them.

## Grounding Story 03 - Correct Result, Poor Release
At fifteen, Tix had reached the age when teachers trusted him with keys they did not trust him with tone.

In July the school posted the scholarship shortlist for Grade 10: four names for the district science track, each attached to a stipend amount everyone pretended not to calculate aloud. The list went up on a Monday, under the bulletin board glass that made every spelling mistake look official.

Tix read it once, then again. He was not checking his own name; his was there, second, exactly where he had expected it. He was checking the averages, because the averages had to match the quarterly sheets and in his experience they never did on first release.

The third name, Ana Villanueva, had an average of `89.4`. It should have been `90.4`. He knew because he had copied the class ranking into his notebook the week before while waiting for table assignment in Computer Lab, and because he had the habit, irritating to others and still useful to him, of copying numbers twice.

The fourth name was Nico Arceo. Nico's father owned a hardware store and parked a new pickup outside the gate on days it did not rain. Nico did not need the stipend in any practical sense, though this was not the same as saying he had no right to it. Rights and needs were often mistaken for one another at school.

At first recess Tix went to the registrar window and asked for verification. The clerk was eating puto and did not look up. She said the list had been signed and could only be revised through the principal's office. He asked for the form. She pointed to a stack of photocopies held down by a stapler that had lost its spring.

He filled out the form in block letters and attached a page from his notebook, the one with the copied rankings. In the "reason for correction" field he wrote: `Transcription discrepancy likely from final averaging row; recommend immediate adjustment to avoid stipend allocation error.`

He took it to their adviser, who was collecting quiz papers and already annoyed by the humidity. She read two lines and told him to leave it on her desk. He asked whether she would sign now. She said she would sign when she had time.

By lunch he was no longer certain she would.

After classes, during flag retreat, the principal read announcements through a microphone that made everyone sound as if they were speaking from inside a drum. Tix stood in the back row with his class and listened to names of athletes, then names of contest qualifiers, then the scholarship list, unchanged.

When the principal reached Nico's name, Tix raised his hand. No one noticed. He raised it higher, then stepped half out of line and said, in a clear voice that carried farther than he intended, "Sir, there is a computation error on line three."

The principal stopped reading. There was a short pause, the sort that invites a joke from people who are not involved. No joke came. The principal called Tix forward, partly because there was no graceful way to continue and partly because principals dislike unresolved interruptions.

Tix handed him the correction form and notebook page. The principal read both, then beckoned their adviser from the side. They spoke in low voices while the students stood in heat and dust, pretending not to stare.

At length the principal returned to the microphone and announced that one average had been incorrectly transcribed, pending immediate confirmation by the registrar. He read Ana's corrected average. He did not read Nico's name again.

There was no applause. There were murmurs, and then, because schools are machines designed to resume, dismissal.

The next day the list was replaced. Ana moved back to third. Nico disappeared from the sheet.

In class, nobody thanked Tix. Nobody accused him, either. What he received was a thin, collective civility, the kind reserved for someone who has done something undeniably correct and socially expensive.

At recess Ana came to his desk and asked if she could borrow his correction fluid. She stood with one hand on the chair back, not sitting.

"You were right," she said.

He nodded.

She waited, then added, "You didn't have to do it like that."

He asked what she meant. The numbers were wrong; the list had to be fixed before fund release; there had been no response at the adviser level.

She looked at him with a patience he did not enjoy receiving. "Now everybody thinks I asked you to fight for me."

"I didn't fight," he said. "I submitted documentation."

She gave a quick, tired smile that might have been amusement and might have been surrender, took the correction fluid, and went back to her seat.

That Friday at club practice, Coach Reyes put him on scoreboard duty and did not mention scholarships. Issa passed by the table and, without looking directly at him, asked for the time. He told her. She nodded and said, "You always know the time," as if this were an achievement of mixed quality.

At home, his mother ironed uniforms while he copied chemistry terms into index cards. She had already heard the story from another parent, with additions. In the retelling, he had "challenged the principal." She asked if this was true.

"No," he said. "I corrected a posted list."

She folded a sleeve along its seam and set the iron upright. "There are ways to correct a list without correcting a person in front of everyone."

He said that if he had waited, stipend processing might have closed.

She considered this. "Probably."

After dinner he took out the old scholarship sheet he had copied, with both versions of the number in different ink. He drew a line between them and wrote, in smaller letters than usual, `Correct result, poor release.`

He looked at the sentence for a while, then tore the page neatly in half and used the blank backs for algebra practice.

## Tix at Present Age - Beliefs, Values, Place in the World
At seventeen, Tix values correctness as an ethical obligation, not a personality quirk. He believes standards should be explicit, measurable, and applied consistently, especially when stakes are uneven. He respects preparation, documentation, and competence, and distrusts decisions that depend on mood, status, or social convenience. He would rather be precise and unpopular than agreeable and vague.

He sees most unfairness as procedural before it is personal: bad records, selective exceptions, weak rollouts, and people protecting face instead of fixing errors. He believes systems can protect people, but only if someone is willing to enforce them with discipline.

He is also beginning to understand a limit in his own method: a correct result can still damage trust if delivered without tact. He knows how to fix the list; he is still learning how to keep people with him while doing it.

## Carry-Forward Truths
- Truth 1: If standards are real, they must survive pressure and favoritism.
- Truth 2: A correct decision can still fail if rollout ignores dignity and timing.
- Truth 3: Documentation protects fairness, but not automatically trust.
