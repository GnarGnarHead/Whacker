## Aya at Seventeen - Beliefs, Values, Place in the World
At seventeen, Aya believes composure is not personality; it is practice. She believes most people are carrying fear they do not name, and that pressure reveals character faster than speeches do.

She values reliability, dignity, restraint, and earned trust. She believes care is only real when it becomes action: showing up, being prepared, helping without turning it into performance. She values privacy too, because not every feeling must be public to be true.

In the world, she sees herself less as a spotlight figure and more as a stabilizer. In the club, she is the person who keeps things from splitting apart. At home, she is often treated as the proof child who must stay on track. She does not think of herself as exceptional. She thinks of herself as accountable.

## Grounding Story 01 - Aya at Six
It rained the sort of rain that does not merely fall, but argues, and it argued all morning against the tin roof above Aya's head.

Aya was six, and six is the exact age at which one believes a ribbon can solve a problem, provided it is tied correctly. She stood on a little stool by the window with two ribbons in her hand and a face of very serious mathematics.

"One for each side," she told herself.

The left side of her hair obeyed immediately. The right side, being of a contrary philosophy, refused in three different ways.

Her mother was bent over a school uniform at the sewing machine, guiding a straight line where yesterday had torn one crooked.

"Anak," her mother said, not looking up, "you are fighting your own hair before breakfast."

"I'm negotiating," Aya said.

This was true. Fighting is loud and fast. Negotiating is quiet and takes forever.

She tried the knot again. The ribbon tilted. She tilted back. They stared at each other, neither willing to be unreasonable.

Outside, a jeepney coughed past the flooded corner, children lifting their socks and stepping from dry tile to dry tile as if the puddles were lava and the world had become a careful game. Aya watched them and tied the knot once more.

This time it sat straight.

Her mother glanced over, nodded once, and returned to the machine. It was not a grand celebration. In their home, success was often greeted by another task.

Aya climbed down from the stool and lined up her things on the table: pencil, eraser, sharpener, coin purse, handkerchief. She put them in her bag in that order. Then she opened the bag and checked.

Rain tapped the window in small impatient fingers.

"You can leave one thing messy," her older cousin said from the doorway, chewing pandesal and watching with the delighted cruelty of an older cousin.

"Which thing?" Aya asked.

He waved his bread in a circle, as if selecting from a menu. "Any thing."

Aya considered this for a full, solemn second and then answered, "No."

By the time they reached school, the hem of her skirt was damp and one sock had betrayed her, but her ribbons were neat, her pencil was sharp, and her handkerchief was folded into a square precise enough to satisfy even a strict geometry teacher.

At recess, another girl cried because her notebook had gotten wet at the edges and gone soft and wrinkled. Aya did not make a speech. She took out her handkerchief, pressed the cover flat, and showed the girl how to place it under the classroom fan without tearing the pages.

"You do things like a grown-up," the girl said.

Aya did not answer right away. She watched the notebook breathe and dry in the wind from the fan.

"No," she said at last. "I do things twice."

That evening, the ribbons came loose before dinner.

She tied them again anyway.

## Grounding Story 02 - Aya at Twelve
When Aya was twelve, she transferred to a larger school where the corridors were long and bright and full of students who seemed to know exactly where to stand.

On her first Monday, she arrived twenty minutes early and still felt late.

The classroom had windows that stuck halfway open. A warm wind pushed in and moved the top corner of every worksheet. Aya chose a desk in the second row, not too near the front, not far enough to look afraid. She put her bag on the chair back and took out a pencil, an eraser, and a blue pen. She lined them side by side on the right edge of the desk. Then she moved them one finger-width left.

Girls at the back were comparing phone cases and laughing over a video. Two boys in front of her argued about basketball shoes. No one looked at her for long, but she felt observed anyway, the way people feel watched in a mirror that is probably only a window.

In Social Studies, the teacher went around the room asking each student to introduce themselves and name one thing they were good at. By the time the roll reached Aya, she had planned her sentence three times and simplified it twice.

"Aya Villanueva," she said. "I transferred from San Lorenzo Elementary."

The teacher nodded. "And what are you good at?"

She had intended to say, "I am organized." It sounded sensible in her head.

What came out was, "I can fold clothes very fast."

There was a small pause, then three boys laughed. Not loudly. Not cruelly enough to be punished. Just enough.

The teacher smiled as if helping. "A practical skill."

Aya nodded and sat down. She kept her face still. She did not look at anyone.

For the rest of the period she copied every word on the board, including the date, though the date was already printed at the top of the handout. She underlined the title once. Then she underlined it again because the first line had drifted upward.

At lunch she ate in a stairwell landing where the light was good and the air smelled faintly of floor wax and soy sauce from somebody's adobo. She could hear the court outside: sneakers, whistles, the soft hollow ping of a table ball from the gym annex.

A girl from her class stopped there to tie her shoe. "You're the folding-clothes girl," she said, not unkindly.

Aya looked up.

"I didn't mean it in a bad way," the girl added quickly. "It's just what I remembered."

Aya nodded, because that was true and also because she did not trust herself yet with any sentence longer than four words.

After classes, rain started as she was crossing the covered walk. It came fast, with no trial drops first. She moved with the others toward the gym to wait it out. The place smelled of varnish and damp concrete. Two older students were rallying at the far table, one in PE shorts, one still in uniform. The ball moved quick and clean between them, not angry, just exact.

Aya watched from the doorway. One of the girls at the table missed long and laughed at herself, then served again.

Miss. Adjust. Serve again.

Aya stood there for nearly ten minutes, listening to the rhythm: contact, floor squeak, contact, breath.

When the rain thinned, she walked home through streets still holding water in the broken parts of the pavement. At home, her mother asked, "How was the first day?"

"Fine," Aya said.

Her mother looked at her for a second longer than usual, then handed her a stack of uniforms to fold.

Aya folded each one flat, lined seams to seams, collars tucked so they would not crush. By the fifth shirt her breathing had settled. By the eighth, she could hear herself think again.

That night, she opened her notebook and wrote three short lines at the top of a blank page:

Say less.
Answer clearly.
Do not rush.

Then she crossed out "Say less" and replaced it with:

Say only what you can stand by.

On Tuesday, she introduced herself to the girl from the stairwell and asked her name before being asked. In class, when called on, she paused once, then answered in a complete sentence. At lunch, she sat with two other girls and listened more than she spoke, but when one of them asked where she had studied before, Aya said the school name steadily and did not apologize for it.

By Friday, no one remembered the folding-clothes answer exactly. They remembered she was neat, she was serious, and she always had a handkerchief when someone spilled water.

That was close enough. It was not the person she was, but it was a person she could work with.

Years later, people would describe her as naturally composed, as if she had been born with that expression and that careful voice. Aya never corrected them. She understood why they thought it. They had not seen the first day, the hot classroom, the small laugh, the way a careless sentence could fasten itself to you.

What they called composure was mostly practice.

## Grounding Story 03 - Aya at Fifteen (Wedding Day)
I was fifteen when my cousin Lani got married, and I woke up before my alarm because my aunties had already started moving around the house.

At five in the morning, family homes sound different. The floorboards have their own language. Rice rinsing in a metal bowl has a rhythm. Someone is always looking for tape. Someone is always asking where the safety pins went.

I ironed my dress in the kitchen because it was the only place with a free outlet and a flat surface. The dress was pale green, sleeveless, with a waist seam that sat one finger too high and made me look younger than I was. My mother said, "It's fine. You'll look neat." In our family, neat is sometimes the same word as beautiful.

By six-thirty, the living room was full of garment bags and perfume and cousins stepping around each other with half-done hair. My lola sat in the corner with her fan and gave instructions nobody admitted to following.

"Don't run in that dress."

"Don't carry hot coffee in that dress."

"Don't breathe too hard in that dress."

She did not say the last one, but it was understood.

At church, the light came in through high windows and made every dust particle visible. I remember that clearly because I kept focusing on the dust instead of the people watching us line up.

I was a junior bridesmaid, which means you are old enough to be useful and young enough to be moved around. Hold this bouquet. Fix that veil. Go find the ring pillow. Stand there. No, not there, there.

Before the ceremony started, I took a tissue and pressed at the makeup under my eyes where it had gone shiny. My cousin Mara looked at me in the mirror and said, "You always look calm. It's annoying."

I said, "I'm not calm. I'm occupied."

She laughed. "Same thing."

It isn't, but I did not argue.

During the vows, one of the flower girls started crying because her shoes were too tight. The photographer hissed from the aisle. My aunt reached for the girl and nearly stepped on the veil. I moved before anyone asked, took the child to the side pew, slipped off her shoes, and gave her my handkerchief to hold. She stopped crying, mostly because she had a task.

When I came back, my tita whispered, "Thank you, anak," with the kind of relief adults use when they are trying not to look panicked in public.

After church came photos. Photos are where families become political.

Who stands in front. Who gets the aisle side. Who is absent and therefore discussed. Who is present and therefore still discussed.

An uncle I only saw at baptisms put his arm around my shoulder and said, "You're getting pretty. Keep it up. Don't be like these lazy kids now."

I smiled the polite smile. The one with no teeth.

At fifteen, I already understood that some compliments are not for you. They are for the person saying them, so they can hear themselves sounding wise.

At the reception hall, the air-conditioning was too cold and the food line was too long. I stood with my plate and listened to relatives ask me versions of the same question.

"What strand will you take?"

"You're still top section, right?"

"Any boyfriend yet?"

"No boyfriend," my mother answered for me once, without turning around.

Nobody asked what I wanted. They asked what I would become.

Near the stage, the emcee kept calling people up for games and dance numbers. My cousins pushed me toward them twice. I shook my head twice. It was not that I hate dancing. I just did not want to perform being easy, or funny, or available, for a room that would evaluate me for sport.

Around three in the afternoon, I escaped to the corridor outside the hall where the signal was weak and the air smelled like wet concrete. I sat on a plastic chair under a framed poster for emergency exits and scrolled through old table tennis clips saved on my phone. Slow serve. Side-spin return. Short push. I watched the same four rallies until I could feel my breathing match them.

My father found me there.

"Hiding?" he asked.

"Cooling down," I said.

He nodded like this made perfect sense. He sat in the chair next to me, loosened his tie, and looked at the parking lot for a while before speaking.

"Your mother worries people will think we're not guiding you enough," he said. "That's why she answers quickly."

I waited.

"It's not always about you," he said. "Sometimes it's about what people do when they don't know what else to do with their fear."

That was one of the only times an adult explained pressure to me without pretending it was advice.

When we went back inside, the program was ending and the crowd had softened into smaller circles. I helped gather envelopes, packed extra cake slices into containers, found two missing chargers, and wrote names on leftover giveaways so nobody would accuse anyone of stealing.

At some point, my cousin Lani hugged me with one arm and said, "You saved this day."

I told her, "No. Everyone did."

That wasn't modesty. It was logistics.

On the drive home, my mother fell asleep against the window. My father drove one-handed and hummed with the radio. I held my shoes in my lap because the straps had rubbed my heel raw, and I pressed my thumb into the tender part until it hurt less.

When we got home, I took off the dress, hung it carefully, and put my handkerchief in warm water with soap to lift out the flower girl's mascara stains.

Then I opened my notebook and wrote one sentence:

If people are afraid, give them something orderly to stand on.

I did not know yet that this line would follow me into almost every room I entered after that. I only knew the wedding had taught me something plain and hard: families can love you and still make you feel inspected; you can feel inspected and still choose to be kind.

The next morning, I went to the gym early and practiced serves alone until my shoulder warmed and my thoughts went quiet.
