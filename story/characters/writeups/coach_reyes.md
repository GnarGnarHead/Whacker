## Grounding Story 01 - The Relief Line
When she was five, Neng Reyes had one pair of school shoes and one pair of slippers, and the slippers were always wet in July. The house was at the end of a narrow lane behind the chapel, where laundry never quite dried and people borrowed salt from each other without returning the same spoon.

That year the rain came too hard for too many days. Water got into the rice sack and made it smell sweet and wrong. Her mother spread the rice on old newspaper and said they would still cook it. Nothing was thrown away if it could still be eaten.

On the third day of rain the barangay announced relief packs at the covered court. Her father had taken the tricycle out before dawn and had not come back. Her mother tied a plastic bag around her own hair, took Neng by the wrist, and carried the baby on her hip. They walked in ankle water, then knee water, then dry concrete near the court where everyone had gathered.

The court was already full. Women stood in one line that had broken into three. Men stood off to the side pretending not to mind waiting. Children ran under the bleachers and came out with muddy knees. At the table near the stage sat two volunteers with a ledger, one box of ballpens, and a megaphone that made every voice sound irritated.

Neng’s mother got them into what looked like the shortest line. It was not the shortest line. It only bent in a way that made it look polite.

After twenty minutes, a woman carrying twins moved to the front. Another woman said she had been there first. A third said nobody had seen either of them. The argument got louder in the slow way arguments do when people are hungry and trying to stay decent.

Neng watched her mother’s face change. Her mother had a face for home, and a face for work, and a face for government offices. This was the government-office face. Calm, tired, ready to be disappointed.

A volunteer shouted that everyone should keep order. Nobody moved.

Neng looked at the ledger, then at the women in front of her, then at the wet concrete where numbers would not stay if written. She took from her pocket a red crayon, already short from being used on margins of old notebooks. She crouched and wrote numbers on the dry side of the low wall beside the line. 1, 2, 3, 4. Then she tapped the wall and pointed to each woman in order.

No one listened at first.

Then one of the older women, the kind who knows everyone’s middle name and debt history, said the child was right and they should stop pretending confusion. She made them stand according to the numbers. She did it briskly, without kindness and without cruelty.

The line became a line.

The twins stopped crying because their mother stopped shouting. The baby on Neng’s mother’s hip fell asleep. The volunteer with the ledger finally began calling names in a voice that had less anger in it.

When it was their turn, Neng’s mother gave the family name and purok number. The volunteer looked at Neng’s wet shirt and muddy legs and added one extra can of sardines without comment. Her mother saw this and did not say thank you out loud, which was how people thanked things they could not name without embarrassment.

They carried the relief pack home together, heavy and damp. On the way, her mother did not praise Neng. She did not scold her either. She only said that if people wanted fairness, someone had to count properly.

That night they ate thin lugaw with sardines and half an onion. Her father came home late and smelled of rain and gasoline. He listened while her mother told him what happened at the court. He laughed once, then looked at Neng as if seeing her from farther away than before.

In the weeks after, Neng began counting everything. Cups before washing. Coins before buying kerosene. Classmates before recess ended. She was not trying to be impressive. She was trying to avoid the kind of confusion that turned adults mean.

Years later, people would call her strict and think they had said something complete. It was never complete. What she trusted was not strictness. It was sequence. One after another. Name after name. So everyone got home with something in their hands.

## Grounding Story 02 - The Biscuit Tin
Neng Reyes was seven the year the school painted the court lines again, but only halfway, so the free-throw arc looked like a moon with one side missing. They held Sports Day anyway. Public schools do not wait for finished paint.

Her teacher chose two captains and told them to pick teams for relay. The captains did what children always do when no one interrupts them: they picked speed first, height second, friends third. By the time they were done, one boy remained beside the wall in his too-large PE shirt, looking down at his shoes as if there might be another instruction hidden there.

Neng knew him. Aris. He was often absent when his chest got bad. He ran with his arms too close to his body, as if protecting something breakable.

No one said his name.

The teacher was busy with the attendance sheet and a parent volunteer asking where to place the water jug. Neng stood in the line and watched Aris hold still beside the wall. She felt something hard in her throat, not sadness exactly, more like annoyance at a mistake no one meant to correct.

On the second round she stepped out, took the class list from the teacher’s desk, and wrote all their names on strips of old worksheet paper. She folded each strip twice. She found an empty biscuit tin in the corner and dropped them in.

When the teacher turned and saw it, she frowned, not because it was wrong, but because it had happened without permission.

Neng said they could draw names instead. The teacher said this was not Neng’s class to run.

The captains looked relieved. The class looked interested. Aris still stood by the wall.

The teacher stared at the tin for a few seconds, then said fine, one round by draw, then back to normal teams. She said it in the tone adults use when they do not want to look persuaded by a child.

Neng held the tin while the captains drew.

Aris got in.

He did not run well. He dropped the baton once and picked it up with both hands. Their team came last by several meters. Still, when he crossed the line, he smiled with all his teeth, which Neng had never seen before. It changed his whole face.

Later, in class, the teacher called Neng to the front. She did not scold her loudly. She gave the softer punishment, the one that stays longer: she told Neng not to embarrass adults in public, even when she meant well.

Neng nodded. She sat down and copied the lesson from the board in careful letters. She kept her eyes on the notebook. She did not cry. She did not feel brave, either. She felt correct and slightly ashamed, which is a complicated feeling for seven.

After class, Aris came to her desk and gave back one of the paper strips she had folded. It had her own name on it. He had kept it in his pocket all afternoon, and it was warm and wrinkled.

He said thank you once and left.

At home that night, Neng told her mother what happened in a flat voice, as if describing weather. Her mother listened while slicing garlic, then asked whether Aris got to run. Neng said yes.

Her mother nodded and kept slicing. She did not praise Neng. She did not say she was right. She only said that people in charge do not always like being helped, and that did not change what needed doing.

For weeks after, Neng noticed who was left standing when groups formed: in games, in line, in class recitation, at the canteen window. She noticed because once you see one child by the wall, you begin seeing them everywhere.

She did not become kinder that year. She became more exact. There was a difference. Kindness could be a mood. Exactness was a method. She began to trust method more.

## Grounding Story 03 - Check-In Ends 7:30
Neng was sixteen and already at school when the janitor unlocked the gym.

She carried two clipboards and a roll of masking tape. The floor smelled like bleach. The lights near the bleachers flickered and stayed that way.

There was a district table tennis meet that day. Nothing big. Four schools. A few parents. A principal who liked to stand where people could see him.

Neng taped the match list to the wall. She wrote the start times with a black marker. Her print was small and straight. At the top she wrote: CHECK-IN ENDS 7:30.

By seven, players had come in. Shoes squeaked. Balls clicked in short rallies. Coaches signed forms and asked where to plug in the electric kettle for coffee.

At 7:29, she called last check.

At 7:31, she put a line through two empty slots.

At 7:40, a boy from her own school came in with his mother. He was breathing hard. His shirt was untucked. His racket case was open at the zipper.

His mother put a hand on the table and smiled at Neng like they were family, because they were.

"We’re here," she said.

Neng looked at the clock above the stage and then at the sheet.

"Check-in closed at 7:30," she said.

The mother laughed. "Traffic. You know how it is. Just add him."

Neng didn’t move.

The boy said, "Come on, Neng."

The gym was loud in that way where everyone can still hear one thing.

Neng picked up the pen, wrote DNS beside his name, and capped the pen.

The mother stared at the paper. "You’re really doing this."

Neng said, "It’s the rule."

The mother took the boy’s arm and pulled him away from the table. Not hard. Hard enough.

They stood near the door for a minute. Then they left.

At eight, the meet started.

A reserve player took the slot and lost in two sets. He apologized three times to no one in particular. Neng handed him water and told him to sit down.

By noon, the air in the gym got thick. Parents fanned themselves with programs. One scoreboard jammed on 10 and had to be slapped to change.

Neng kept carrying forms from table to table. She taped one corner back down when it peeled. She wrote scores. She checked names twice.

At three, her team coach came over while she was stacking chairs.

"That was rough with your cousin," he said.

Neng nodded.

"You did right," he said, then looked toward the exit. "Didn’t make it easier."

She nodded again.

That night there was a family gathering for her uncle’s birthday. Pancit in foil trays. Karaoke too loud in the living room. Plastic chairs set in rows that no one kept.

Her aunt passed by Neng once and did not stop.

Later, in the kitchen, Neng’s father was rinsing plates.

He said, "Your tita is upset."

Neng leaned against the sink.

He kept rinsing. "Maybe she’ll get over it."

Neng said nothing.

He put a plate on the rack and said, "You could have bent it."

She looked at him.

He shrugged. "Maybe not."

After everyone left, she swept the floor and found three bottle caps, a spoon, and one earring back.

Before bed, she opened her school notebook and wrote one line on the last page:

Rules are easy when they help you.

She closed the notebook and put it under her pillow.

The next week, her cousin spoke to her again. He asked for notes in Math. She handed him the notes. He said thanks. That was all.

They never talked about the meet. They didn’t need to.

Neng kept showing up early. She kept bringing tape and two pens. She kept writing times no one liked until they needed them.

## Grounding Story 04 - Replay It
At twenty-one, Neng spent Saturdays at the municipal gym, keeping score for matches no one important would remember. The table was set too close to the wall, so every hard return ended in an apology and a laugh that was only half a laugh. By noon the heat had turned the room metallic; the ball sounded thinner each rally, like it was wearing out in the air.

In the second match after lunch, two men stopped playing and turned to her at the same time, each insisting the point belonged to him. She looked at the sheet, then at their faces, and understood that neither of them was really asking what happened. They were asking to be confirmed. She said, quietly, "Replay it." They stared, then nodded, and took their positions without another word.

The point they replayed was short and ugly. One clipped the net, the other overreached and missed. They accepted it because the game had moved, and movement was enough.

Afterward, Neng folded the score sheets into a square and put them in her bag beside a borrowed library book she had not opened. Outside, rain had started without warning. She stood under the awning, smelling dust turn to mud, and waited for it to become her turn to run.

## Grounding Story 05 - Provisional Entry
By twenty-four, Neng had the keys to the gym office and the habit of arriving before sunrise, when the court still smelled of soap and old wood. She liked that first hour because nothing had happened yet. The scoreboard lights were dark, the paper brackets unmarked, and every player was still, for a little while, equally possible.

It was a Saturday meet for juniors, not important enough for newspapers, important enough for parents. By seven-thirty the hallway was crowded with shoes left in no particular order and mothers holding forms in plastic folders. Neng sat behind the folding table with the check-in list and a pen that kept skipping on damp paper.

A girl from San Isidro arrived with her uncle, both of them wet to the knee from the rain. The girl had everything except one signature: guardian consent on the back page. Her uncle said the mother was at the district hospital and had signed it at home, but the page had gotten left behind in the rush. He said this in a patient voice that was close to breaking.

At sixteen, Neng would have crossed the name out and moved to the next line. She could feel that old movement in her hand even now, neat and automatic. Instead she asked them to wait and went looking for the rulebook in the drawer where people put things they expected never to use.

There was a clause she remembered only dimly, a line about provisional entry in exceptional circumstances, with identity verified and consent confirmed by call. She made the call herself, wrote the mother’s full name as it was spoken to her, then the time: 7:42. She had the school clerk sign as witness. She stamped the form and set it on top of the pile.

The girl played at nine and lost in straight sets, 11-5, 11-7. No upset, no miracle. She thanked Neng anyway, with the stiff politeness of someone raised not to make trouble.

After the meet, while the janitor stacked monobloc chairs and rain tapped at the roof again, Neng closed the register and sat for a minute with both hands flat on the table. She did not feel proud. She felt something steadier than that, and less exciting.

She thought that rules were still what kept people from being pushed aside by louder voices. But she had learned, by then, that some rules had doors in them, and that opening one properly was not the same thing as letting everything collapse.

## Grounding Story 06 - Started
At thirty-four, Neng took a half-day leave and went to the Civil Registrar with a clear envelope that held everything in order: death certificate, marriage certificate, barangay clearance, photocopies of IDs, two 2x2 photos her mother insisted might be needed. The guard wrote 83 on the back of her hand with marker. The board above the counter said 61 and stayed there for almost an hour.

When her number was called, the clerk read the forms without looking up. He tapped one line with his nail. Her father’s middle initial on the death certificate was T. On the marriage contract it was S. "Needs correction," he said. "Affidavit. Two witnesses. Return complete."

Neng asked where to file it. The clerk pointed to a printed sheet taped to the window, its corners gray with handling. She copied each requirement into her notebook, one under another, not because writing made it easier but because it kept the work from becoming formless.

At the photocopy stall outside, she saw her aunt in the next line, holding a sleeping baby. The aunt asked what happened, listened, and said, "I can sign. I knew your father before any office did."

By four they were back upstairs. By four-thirty they had one witness. At four-forty-eight the second witness arrived damp from rain, apologizing to everyone and no one. At four-fifty-six the affidavit was stamped. The clerk slid the folder back and said processing could take three weeks, maybe four.

On the jeep home, her aunt sat beside her and kept one hand on the envelope as if it might slide away. Traffic moved in short starts. Rainwater held the red brake lights in long thin lines.

"Your father hated paperwork," the aunt said.

Neng looked at the folder. "He kept every receipt," she said.

At home, her mother was waiting at the table with two cups of coffee gone lukewarm. She asked, "Done?"

Neng set the envelope down carefully, so the corners would not bend.

"Started," she said.

## Carry-Forward Truths
- Truth 1: Fairness must be process-backed, not personality-backed.
- Truth 2: You do the right thing even when it strains family and social ties.
- Truth 3: Rules are strongest when they include accountable exceptions.

## Values and Traits (Age 34)
- Values fairness through sequence, documentation, and repeatable process.
- Rejects favoritism, including for relatives and insiders.
- Protects overlooked people through systems, not speeches.
- Treats preparation as care: early arrival, backups, clear procedures.
- Chooses durable outcomes over public approval.
- Uses lawful discretion when strict rule application would be unjust.
- Calm under pressure, emotionally restrained, and difficult to rattle.
- Precise, practical, and reliable; not performative.
- Can be perceived as cold when she is actually boundary-driven.

## Scene Usage Notes
- Dialogue tone implications: short sentences, exact words, no theatrics, minimal self-explanation.
- Match behavior implications: disciplined structure, clear standards, practical corrections, quiet accountability.
- Caricature traps to avoid: do not write her as punitive, robotic, or morally superior.
