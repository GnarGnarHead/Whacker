## Grounding Story 01 - The Tin Top
Benji was four that rainy season, in the small preschool room behind the barangay hall where the floor stayed damp near the back wall and the fan worked only on one speed, too fast for paper and too slow for heat. The teacher kept the toys in a blue basin with a crack taped over by packing tape that had gone the color of tea.

There was one tin top in that basin, painted red long ago, with a steel point at the bottom and a short string tied to a nail at the head. The paint had chipped away in places, but it still caught the eye. When it spun cleanly it made a soft humming sound that drew children toward it even if they had been fighting over something else a moment before.

Benji wanted the top from the first week. He wanted it in a hard, private way. He was too small to take it from the bigger boys and too cautious to ask while they were watching. He stood close enough to see how they wrapped the string and how quickly they pulled. He watched the top wobble, settle, and hold its balance. He watched faces turn toward it.

At four, he had already learned that wanting was not enough. In his house, wanting had to wait its turn behind rent, rice, and electric bills written in pencil by a landlord who did not smile. His mother left early, came back tired, and spoke in practical sentences. His older cousin borrowed things and returned some of them. Nobody asked where fairness lived. They asked where the missing coins had gone.

One Thursday afternoon the room was louder than usual. Rain was hitting the roof in short hard bursts. The children had been told to stay inside for recess, and they turned the room into a weather system of their own. A cup of powdered milk went over near the low table. Somebody stepped in it and tracked white footprints across the cement. The teacher, already wet at the shoulders from hanging laundry under the eaves, began wiping the floor with old T-shirts cut into squares.

The top was in play by then. Two boys were using it near the door, taking turns badly. The younger one cried each time the older one held it one beat too long. The crying was not about pain. It was about time, and possession, and being seen.

Benji stood by the shelf and watched all of this as if he were waiting for instructions. He had a face adults liked in moments like that: quiet, alert, easy to place on the side of order. He knew this, though he would not have had words for it. He only knew that when he stood still and looked careful, grown-ups looked back differently.

The teacher asked, in a tired voice, who had spilled the milk. No one answered. She asked again, and this time Benji lifted his hand and gave the name of the older boy with the top.

He did not shout. He did not accuse dramatically. He said it softly, almost helpfully, and he pointed once to the white footprints near the door, where everyone's prints had crossed everyone else's. It was enough.

The teacher took the top away from the boy and sat him near the wall for timeout. She wiped his hands with a damp cloth and told him he had lost toy privilege for the rest of the session. He protested, then cried, then stopped when he saw no one else was joining him. The room settled in the way rooms do when authority chooses a version and everyone agrees to live inside it.

The teacher turned to Benji and, needing one child to be simple in that moment, placed the top in his hand and said he could hold it until cleanup was done because he was being cooperative.

He held it with both palms like something warm. When the room thinned toward pickup time, he took it into the corridor and crouched by the wall where the concrete was smoother. He wrapped the string the way he had watched the others do, pulled badly the first time, then better, then better. On the fourth try, the top stood and hummed in place, steady and proud, and he felt a small lift in his chest that had nothing to do with guilt.

His mother came late that day, smelling faintly of cooking oil and rainwater. She apologized to the teacher, thanked her for waiting, and took Benji by the wrist as they crossed the hall. He did not mention the boy or the milk or the top. He told her only that he had practiced spinning.

That night, while his mother counted change on the table and crossed one item off next day's list, Benji sat on the floor with a bottle cap and tried to make it spin on its edge. It would not. It fell and fell and fell again.

Still, something had fixed itself in him that day. Not kindness. Not cruelty, exactly. Something colder and more useful: the idea that if he stayed calm, spoke at the right moment, and offered adults a plausible story, the room would often tilt in his direction.

He was four. It was a small incident in a wet room no one important would remember. But later, when he learned to make serves break awkwardly and to win points that looked accidental from the other side of the table, the motion felt familiar. He had been practicing that angle a long time.

## Grounding Story 02 - The Borrow Log
Long after the paper should have been thrown away, the borrow log stayed in the drawer of the sports cabinet, folded once and then folded again, so the names ran into the crease and became difficult to read. Benji knew this because, at nine, he had written the names himself in careful block letters, and at fifteen he checked once to see if they were still there. They were, though the ink had turned weak and brown, as if it had changed its mind.

In Grade 4, their school received three decent table tennis paddles from a municipal youth program, which was the sort of donation that came with a photograph and no replacements. The paddles had black-and-red rubbers that smelled faintly of glue and came in thin sleeves that looked expensive enough to be important. The old club paddles, with splintered handles and dead rubber, were pushed to one side like relatives no one had time for.

Because there were only three, a system appeared. Systems appear quickly when everyone can count the shortage. The adviser made a borrow sheet on yellow pad paper. One name at a time, one period at a time, return checked by signature. It sounded fair and therefore unarguable.

Benji was good with lists. Adults noticed this and mistook it for character, which often happens in schools.

He was given custody of the clipboard during after-class practice. He stood near the scorer's table, called names, marked return times, and pretended not to care when better players took longer turns than assigned. On paper, everyone had twelve minutes. In the room, twelve minutes stretched for boys who had confident fathers and narrowed for boys who looked down when spoken to.

Noel was one of the confident boys. He was not cruel, only assured, and assurance itself can occupy more space than malice. He had a forehand everyone praised and a way of arriving late that somehow became evidence of talent. Teachers said he was naturally competitive. Other children said he was lucky. He preferred either explanation to the third one, which was practice.

Benji did practice. He used one of the dead paddles and made the ball spin off the corner of a desk in the back classroom where no one came after four-thirty. He had learned that if he brushed thinly under the ball it would jump awkwardly on the second bounce, not pretty, but useful. It was the kind of shot that embarrassed people because it made them look careless.

He wanted one of the good paddles long enough to learn what his spin could do on real rubber. Wanting, in that room, had to pass through the borrow sheet.

For several weeks he played by the system he maintained. He accepted scraps of time. He corrected return marks when others forgot. He handed paddles over with both hands, careful not to smudge the small number stickers on the handle butt. Teachers liked that. No one likes a child who wants too visibly.

The week before intramurals, rain moved in and did not leave. Practice shifted indoors entirely. Shoes squeaked on damp concrete. The fan stirred the smell of wet socks and floor polish. They had one table, one working net clip, and twenty students with opinions.

On Thursday, during the noisiest hour, Paddle 2 went missing.

It disappeared between Noel's turn and the next name, which happened to be Benji's. The sleeve was found under the bleacher bench, empty. The borrow sheet showed Noel's return unchecked. Noel said he had put the paddle on the table edge. Two classmates said they had seen it there. Three others said they had not. In six minutes the room had become a tribunal with no evidence and excellent memory.

The adviser did what tired adults do under time pressure. She stopped play and began searching bags.

Benji stood by the clipboard with his face arranged into concern. He offered practical suggestions in a quiet voice. He asked whether they should check the cleaning closet and the stage storage first because loose equipment was often moved there during rain. This sounded like procedure and therefore wisdom.

Paddle 2 was eventually found in the stage storage behind a rolled tarpaulin and a broken music stand, dry and clean, as if it had been placed there by someone who did not intend it to get stepped on. Noel was warned for carelessness, and carelessness in schools has a long afterlife. It attaches to a name and follows it through unrelated incidents.

No punishment was given beyond that, because the paddle had been recovered and the day needed to continue. Continuation is always more important than certainty in small institutions.

By Friday, the adviser adjusted the borrow system. Certain students, she said, could no longer be trusted with unsupervised handling. She assigned Paddle 2 to the students who, in her words, respected equipment protocols. Benji was first on that list.

The change looked administrative. It felt personal.

He played with Paddle 2 for twenty clean minutes after class while rain hammered the roof and most students waited by the gate for pickups. The rubber was livelier than he expected. His brush contacts caught and lifted in ways the old paddle never allowed. His awkward second bounce became sharper, meaner, difficult in exactly the way he liked.

He wrote the borrow and return times neatly afterward, adding his initials in the check column with a pen that had begun to skip. The sheet looked orderly. The incident became record, then memory, then an anecdote about equipment discipline told by teachers who preferred moral clarity to unresolved details.

At home that night his mother asked why he looked pleased. He said practice had gone better. She nodded, counted change from her apron pocket, and asked him to list what they needed from the corner store before prices moved again on Monday.

He made the list in a straight column: cooking oil, soy sauce, detergent, sugar by the quarter kilo.

Under the list, without thinking much about it, he wrote one extra line that was not for the store and not for his mother.

Never touch what can be misplaced instead.

Years later, seeing the folded borrow log in the cabinet drawer, he did not unfold it all the way. He did not need to. He remembered enough: the rain, the tarpaulin, the adviser's relieved face when the paddle reappeared, and the peculiar comfort of a story that sounded reasonable to everyone who needed the day to keep moving.

## Benji at Present Age - Beliefs, Values, Place in the World
At sixteen, Benji believes outcomes matter more than clean methods, provided the path stays deniable. He values leverage, timing, and narrative cover: who gets trusted, who gets tagged as careless, and who appears cooperative when adults are tired. He reads institutions as practical machines that prefer continuity over truth.

He is not grandly malicious. He is small-scale and opportunistic, drawn to advantages that look accidental from the outside. He avoids direct confrontation, especially with stronger personalities, and prefers nudges, insinuations, and procedural shadows.

His private insecurity is status. He wants access to quality tools, better opponents, and social legitimacy, but does not trust open competition to grant those things fairly. So he builds edge through indirection, then tells himself he is only being realistic.

## Carry-Forward Truths
- Truth 1: In crowded systems, plausible stories often beat strict certainty.
- Truth 2: Indirect control feels safer than direct confrontation.
- Truth 3: If he can steer both the ball and the narrative, he feels less exposed.
