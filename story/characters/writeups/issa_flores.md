## Grounding Story 01 - Two Kinds of Quiet
At eight, Issa learned there were two kinds of quiet in a house: the one that meant sleep, and the one that meant waiting.

On Thursdays her mother ran a small canteen out of the front window, selling boiled eggs, instant noodles, and soft drinks from a cooler that never quite shut. Issa's job was to rinse glasses and stack them upside down on a towel that smelled faintly of detergent and onion.

Her brother, Paolo, was eleven and had a cough that came in the rainy months and stayed longer than anyone wanted. When the cough was bad, he leaned one shoulder against the doorframe and watched the street instead of playing. He said he was fine in a voice that wanted no follow-up.

That evening the rain had been coming since four. By six the lane was half water and the tricycles sent up fans of it when they turned in. Men stood under eaves and shouted to each other across the rain as if volume could make distance smaller.

At half past six, Paolo took two steps toward the kitchen and sat down on the floor, slowly, as if he had decided to tie his shoe and then forgotten the shoe. He put one hand to his chest and breathed in short pulls.

Their mother said his name once, then again sharper.

Issa was standing by the sink with a glass in her hand. She set it down without looking and went to the gate before being told.

The clinic was five minutes away on dry days and farther in rain. The alley light by the chapel had gone out, and the puddles were deep enough to hide the holes beneath. Issa ran to the corner, then to the sari-sari store, then to Mang Nestor's tricycle stand where three drivers were playing cards with the engine off.

She said, "Kuya Paolo can't breathe right. Please come now."

One driver said the road by the creek was flooded.

Another said wait for the rain to ease.

Mang Nestor folded his cards, stood up, and took his key from the nail by the post.

"Bring a towel," he told Issa. "Seat is wet."

She ran back with the towel and her mother's coin purse and found her brother on the chair now, shirt open at the neck, eyes closed but not sleeping. Their mother had a hand on his shoulder and the other on his back, rubbing in small circles as if she could remind his lungs what to do.

In the tricycle, Issa sat in front with Mang Nestor and held the metal bar with both hands when they hit water. Behind her she could hear her mother saying, "In through your nose. Slow. Slow," and then saying it again when slow did not arrive.

At the clinic, the nurse put a mask on Paolo and turned a valve until the hiss came. The room smelled of alcohol and damp curtains. Issa stood by the doorway and counted his breaths because counting was something she could do that did not require permission.

After a while the breaths lengthened. His shoulders dropped. He opened one eye and looked at her.

"Don't tell school," he said.

She nodded.

On the way home, the rain had thinned. Their mother sat very straight in the tricycle, one hand still on Paolo's arm as if a person could float away if not held.

Back at the house, she opened the canteen window again though it was late and no one was buying. She needed to wipe the counter and line up the sachets and close the cash tin properly. She moved carefully, like someone putting dishes away after guests had gone.

Issa dried the last two glasses and set them upside down on the towel.

Before bed, Paolo asked for water. She brought it and waited while he drank.

"You ran fast," he said.

She shrugged, because praise made her uncomfortable in a way panic did not.

In the morning she went to school and told no one. At recess she stood between two girls arguing over a ballpoint pen and said they could both use it if one wrote now and one after lunch. They accepted this as if it had been their idea.

Years later, people would call Issa easy with people, and she would let them. What she remembered was the rain, the drivers at the cards, and how quickly a room could change when one person could not breathe. It was then she began to prefer movement over noise, and solutions over who deserved what first.

## Grounding Story 02 - Old Slippers
Last month, Issa came home wet to the knees and found her mother at the table with the cash tin open, counting coins into stacks that leaned and fell over, then leaned again. Paolo was on the floor with the standing fan laid sideways, talking to it as if it were a stubborn person. Nobody asked where she'd been right away, which in their house was the first kindness. When her mother finally looked up, she said, "Coach called," and Issa felt her whole body tighten before she could stop it. "Not trouble," her mother added, seeing that. "She asked if we had old slippers." Paolo laughed once and said, "Our sister is running disaster response now," and Issa wanted to tell him to shut up, but she didn't, because he was already standing to check under the sink for pairs that still had soles.

## Grounding Story 03 - Keep One Spare Pair
That night, after dinner, Paolo carried the rescued slippers to the sink as if they were evidence in a trial, and Issa stood beside him with the dish brush while their mother read off judgments from the doorway. Blue pair: acceptable, though the left strap was holding on by tape and prayer. Green pair: still good if nobody ran in them. A lone black sandal, no partner: hopeless, set aside for future flowerpot duty.

The fan, now reassembled, worked only on low and made a clicking sound every few turns, like a person trying not to interrupt and failing. Paolo said he'd fix it properly on Sunday. Their mother said he had said that last Sunday too. He said this time he meant it in writing.

Issa washed mud off the soles and lined the slippers upside down along the windowsill. She didn't say much. She was replaying the moment at practice when Coach's name had come up and her chest had gone tight before the sentence was finished. She hated that about herself, the jumpiness, as if she were always bracing for blame.

When she opened her allowance envelope in her room, she found she was short twenty pesos, spent on hot bread and chocolate drink for someone else's little brother. She moved one folded bill from snacks to fare and wrote a small `-20` on the flap, then `Friday` beside it, so she wouldn't pretend later that she had forgotten.

From the kitchen came the sound of her mother rinsing rice for tomorrow and Paolo arguing with the fan again. It was oddly comforting, the same small arguments in the same small rooms. Before bed, Issa took her notebook and wrote one line under CLUB:

`Keep one spare pair by the door.`

Then she closed the notebook and put it under her pillow, where she kept things she didn't want explained.

## Issa at Present Age - Beliefs, Values, Place in the World
At fifteen, Issa believes care is practical before it is verbal. She values timing, coordination, and making sure people have what they need in the moment they need it. She trusts people who follow through and has little patience for performative concern that produces no result.

She reads social pressure quickly and tends to move first: de-escalate, redistribute load, keep the group usable. She does not think of this as leadership, only maintenance. But in practice, she is often the person who keeps the room from splitting.

She also carries a private reflex of bracing for blame. Even when she acts effectively, she anticipates fallout. That tension makes her both highly responsive and sometimes too hard on herself.

## Voice Artifact - Diary Entry (Last Night)
Thursday night

I came home wet to the knees again. Mud on my skirt, hair stuck to my neck, whole body tired in that way where you feel like you're still standing even when you sit down.

Ma said, "Coach called," and my chest went tight before I could even think. Then she said, "Not trouble," and I felt stupid for bracing. I still do that. I hate it.

Coach just asked if we had spare slippers for younger players. Paolo laughed and said I'm running disaster response now, but he still got up and checked under the sink. Typical.

We sorted the old pairs after dinner like they were official equipment:
- blue pair still usable
- green pair usable if no one sprints
- one black orphan sandal, RIP

I washed mud off everything and lined them by the window. The fan is still making that click-click noise every few turns. Paolo says he'll fix it "properly" on Sunday. We'll see.

I checked my allowance envelope in my room. I'm short 20 pesos from buying bread and hot drink earlier. Moved money from snacks to fare and wrote `-20 / Friday` on the flap so I don't pretend I forgot.

I keep thinking about how people assume this stuff is "being nice." It isn't that simple. Most of the time it's just logistics. Who has slippers. Who ate. Who gets where. What breaks first.

Wrote one line in my notebook before bed:

`Keep one spare pair by the door.`

That's it. That's the rule tonight.

## Carry-Forward Truths
- Truth 1: Move first, explain second, if someone is in immediate need.
- Truth 2: Care means logistics: food, dry gear, timing, and coverage.
- Truth 3: A group holds when pressure is redistributed, not ignored.
