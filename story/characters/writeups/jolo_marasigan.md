## Grounding Story 01 - The Iron Drum
By four, Jolo already understood weight.

A full pail bit your fingers. A sack of rice did not care how angry you were. Ice in burlap melted and bled cold through your shirt. He learned this at the public market where his mother sold fish under a roof of patched tin, with rain always testing the nails.

That afternoon the sky came down early, black by three. Wind shoved through the aisles and turned the hanging tarps to sails. Men climbed stools to tie them down. Women threw old tires on the corners of tables so nothing would lift and go skidding into the gutter.

Jolo sat on an overturned crate, heel kicking the wood, a spoon in one hand, a fish-scale bucket in the other, making his own weather.

Then the wind hit harder. One side of the market roof lifted and clanged back. Somebody shouted. Two stalls over, a stack of ice boxes shifted, slid, and came loose. The top box struck his mother's table leg. The table jerked. A steel cart rolled sideways and pinned her thigh against the concrete post.

She made a sound he had never heard from her. Not a curse, not a yell. A sound like something tearing.

"Jolo!" she shouted, teeth bared. "Get help!"

He ran one step and stopped. The aisle was white with rain and bodies moving the wrong direction, everyone busy with their own emergency. Tarps snapped. A child cried near the onion sacks. A man wrestled a bamboo pole that wanted to become a spear.

His mother tried to shift her leg and couldn't. Her hands were slick with fish water and blood from somewhere he could not see.

"Jolo!" she said again, and this time his name cracked in the middle.

He looked for someone bigger. No one was looking at them.

On the floor beside the weighing scale was a short iron gaff used for pulling ice. He grabbed it with both hands, stumbled under its weight, then swung it down on the empty steel drum by the post.

The drum rang out, sharp and ugly.

He hit it again.
Again.
Again.

Each strike shook his arms to the shoulder. The gaff was too heavy, the arc sloppy, but the sound cut through rain and shouting like a blade through plastic twine.

Heads turned.

He kept hitting. He did not call out. He just hit and hit until a butcher from the meat lane came running, then two porters, then the cigarette seller's son. They lifted the cart enough for his mother to drag her leg free. She slid to the wet floor and breathed in short, furious pulls.

When it was over, she sat on a crate with her skirt soaked black to the knee and held his face in both hands, fish scales stuck to her wrist like silver commas.

"You did right," she said, voice still rough. "You made them hear."

By evening, the storm had moved inland. The market smelled of mud, diesel, and thawed ice. His mother wrapped her thigh with an old bandage and sold the rest of the fish at a loss because tomorrow's fish always comes, whether you are ready or not.

At home she told the story to his father without softening it: the roof lifting, the cart, the drum, the boy with both hands on iron. His father laughed once, not because it was funny, but because fear often leaves men with one sound and no better one behind it.

After that, whenever something jammed in the house, a window, a rusted lock, the stubborn back gate in rainy season, Jolo reached for force first. Not always wisely. Sometimes not well. But first.

Long before he learned spin or angle or patience, he learned that panic was noise, and noise could be beaten into a signal if you struck hard enough.

## Grounding Story 02 - The Edge Ball
At nine, Jolo had already learned there were two ways to win a point, the way everyone saw and the way everyone remembered.

It was August, a Sunday league at the covered court behind the chapel, with paper brackets taped to a post and names written in marker that bled when the rain came sideways. They had one real table and one that leaned half an inch toward the gutter side, so balls that died short looked like they had opinions.

Jolo entered in the under-twelve bracket because his mother had brought fish early, sold enough by ten, and told him to go run his legs before lunch. He went in slippers, carrying a borrowed paddle in a plastic bag, with a bottle cap in his pocket to serve as a coin if they needed to toss.

By noon he'd won two matches mostly by force, the sort of forehand he believed in then, full shoulder, full noise, no thought for the ball after contact. Men by the post nodded at those shots, and boys who had lost stood close and pretended they weren't watching. It was the first kind of approval he understood perfectly.

The third match was against a boy from San Roque named Emil, smaller, quieter, with a clean serve that landed short and jumped to the elbow if you reached too early. Emil didn't hit hard. He hit where there was no one.

By 9-all, the court had gone still in that irritating way crowds do when they think they are being respectful but are really trying to hear something break.

Jolo won the next point with a serve Emil read late. 10-9. Match point.

On the next rally Emil served short. Jolo stepped in and drove the ball hard to Emil's backhand corner. Emil got there, barely, and lifted a high return that dropped near the edge on Jolo's side. Jolo swung before the bounce rose and sent the ball long.

He shouted "Out" before anyone else said anything.

Emil, still near the table, said softly, "It touched."

Jolo said no. He said it fast, certain, loud enough to make certainty for everyone else.

The two men keeping score looked at each other. One had been watching the bracket, one the tricycle lane. Neither had seen clearly. The older one, who knew Jolo's uncle, shrugged and marked the point for Jolo.

Match.

There was no argument after that. Emil picked up his towel, said "Good game" in a voice that sounded like a sentence copied from somewhere, and stepped away. Jolo took the handshake, nodded once, and let the noise from the sideline carry him.

He made finals and lost them 11-6 to a left-handed boy who rolled serves into his forehand body and never gave him a clean swing. He got a plastic medal anyway for second place, blue ribbon, tin disk printed with a torch that looked like a spoon.

At home, his mother was washing knives at the sink and asked how it went.

"Second," he said, and put the medal on the table where she could see it without turning.

She wiped her hands, looked at the medal, and said that was good. Then she asked, without emphasis, "Did you win all your points?"

He said yes too quickly and knew she knew.

She did not lecture him. She put rice into bowls and called him to carry one to his father in the back room.

His father looked at the medal, laughed once, and said, "Second means you almost had it. Next time hit harder early."

Jolo nodded. It was the answer he wanted in one way and not in another.

On Tuesday, after class, he saw Emil by the sari-sari store counting coins for a bag of cooking oil and one sachet of soy sauce. Emil saw him too and lifted his chin once, not hostile, not friendly. Just acknowledgment, the kind boys use when they've already decided what another boy is worth.

Jolo said, "Your short serve is good."

Emil said, "Your call was loud."

Then Emil took the oil and walked off toward the side street.

That night Jolo took the borrowed paddle to the small table outside and bounced the ball against it until dark, first hard, then softer, trying to find the place where contact made direction instead of noise. He kept hearing the word "touched," not as accusation now, just as fact, and he hated the feeling of it sticking.

By the end of the week he had decided, in the private courtroom children keep for themselves, that next time he would win the same point clean, with no voice needed. It was not repentance exactly. It was pride in a different shirt.

He kept the medal in a drawer with old school IDs and a dead whistle. He told the story a few times that month and left out the edge ball each time, which was also a way of remembering it.

## Grounding Story 03 - The Floodlight Game
At twelve, Jolo grew faster than their house.

By June his school shorts stopped at the wrong place above his knees and his elbows seemed to arrive in rooms before the rest of him. His mother said boys like that broke chairs and promises if no one kept them tired.

So after classes he went to the court near the tricycle terminal, where the concrete held old cracks full of dark water and the painted lines had faded into suggestion. They called it a table tennis court because someone had dragged in a table years before and never moved it back out. At night, a floodlight from the barangay hall leaned over the place and made everybody look harder than they were.

A local money game started there every Thursday in rainy season. Not big money, but enough that boys watched and men stayed. One table. One envelope. Winner takes most, runner-up enough for rice and kerosene.

Jolo was not old enough to enter. Which meant, by local logic, he entered if he could pay.

He paid with two days' lunch money and one ten-peso coin his mother had left on the shelf for jeep fare. He told himself he would replace it before she counted.

The bracket was short that night because rain had come in hard from five onward and the road by the creek was already half under. Players arrived wet to the back, slippers slapping mud on the court edge. Somebody put cardboard under one table leg to stop the wobble. Somebody else lit a cigarette off a stove match and used the match to check wind direction, like the ball cared.

Jolo got through his first game on power and noise. He hit through spin, through bad bounces, through his own balance. The older boys by the chain-link fence shouted his name like they'd discovered him themselves.

In the second game he faced a tricycle driver named Ramil, who played with an old blade and a rubber patch on his thumb where the handle had worn skin over years. Ramil served short and ugly, the ball skidding low, then dead. Jolo lost the first set 11-4 before he understood where the points were going.

He won the second 11-9 by standing closer and taking everything early, arm burning, lungs hot. By then it was after nine and the rain had turned from sheets to needles.

The deciding set reached 8-8. On the next rally Jolo stepped in for a forehand, planted wrong on the wet edge, and his left foot went out from under him. He hit the table corner with his hip, then the floor with the back of his head. The sound was wooden and final, like a coconut dropped on packed earth.

For one second nobody moved.

Then everyone moved.

Ramil was first. He knelt, one hand on Jolo's shoulder, and told him not to get up. The voice was rough but careful. Someone brought a towel. Someone said call a tricycle. Someone said don't move his neck. Somebody else said he was fine, look, he's cursing already.

Jolo tried to sit and the court spun sideways. The floodlight split into two moons and then one. He tasted iron. Not blood exactly. Something like it.

They got him to the barangay clinic in the back of a tricycle with no side cover, rain coming in slantwise. Ramil held his wrist the whole way to keep him from standing up when the vehicle hit potholes.

At the clinic, Nurse Delia asked who his guardian was. Jolo said his mother was at market. Delia made a face that said she'd heard this answer too many times from too many boys who believed themselves indestructible.

By ten-thirty his mother came in wearing her market apron over a dry blouse and no slippers, just heel-strapped sandals she only used for church. She looked at the bump on his head, then at the form on the desk, then at him.

No shouting. That made it worse.

On the form, under "cause of injury," Delia had written:
`Amateur match, wet court, unsupervised minor.`

His mother signed where told and said very little on the walk home except: "You spent fare money to play for money."

He said he would replace it.

She said, "With what?"

He had no answer big enough.

By Sunday he was back at the court, not playing, just watching from the terminal wall with his hood up and his hands in his pockets. The older boys called out that he had gone soft. One asked if he needed his mother's permission slip to serve.

Jolo said nothing. He watched Ramil play a teenager from the next barangay and win with five straight short serves the boy kept lifting high. No force. No celebration. Just pressure where the other player had nowhere clean to stand.

After the game Jolo walked over and asked, "How do you make it die like that?"

Ramil looked at him for a second and said, "You want to hit hard all the time. Hard is expensive."

Then he put a ball on the table edge and showed him, with two fingers and almost no motion, how to touch under and across so the bounce had bite instead of noise.

Jolo practiced that serve for a week with a cracked plastic ball and a piece of plywood balanced on hollow blocks behind their house. He still hit hard when angry, still swung too early when crowded, still felt his chest swell when people shouted his name. But now there was one new thing in him: the knowledge that force without control did not just lose points. Sometimes it put your mother in church sandals at ten-thirty at night, signing forms she should never have had to see.

## Jolo at Present Age - Beliefs, Values, Place in the World
At seventeen, Jolo values directness, loyalty, and visible effort. He believes people earn respect by showing up and taking pressure head-on, not by explaining themselves well. He is skeptical of talk that is not backed by action and has little patience for posturing.

He came up in environments where mistakes cost money, time, and dignity, so he reads competitive play through that lens. For him, force is not only style; it is certainty under stress. But he has learned, by experience, that unstructured force can become waste or harm.

He now carries a working rule he would not phrase poetically: power has to be usable. He still wants to dominate points physically, but he is increasingly willing to choose control when the situation demands it.

## Carry-Forward Truths
- Truth 1: In a crisis, make yourself impossible to ignore.
- Truth 2: Winning by noise leaves residue; clean execution lasts longer.
- Truth 3: Force without control is expensive for everyone around you.
